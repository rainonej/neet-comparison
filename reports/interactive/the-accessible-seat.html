A fair paper is not a fair race.

NEET is India’s once-a-year entrance exam for undergraduate medicine. In 2024, 2.33 million candidates competed for roughly 130,000 MBBS places through a three-hour multiple-choice test.

A leaked paper triggered the current protests. But suppose the exam had been perfectly clean. Would the ordinary race be fair?

No. Scroll to see why ↓

01 · Orientation

NEET in 20 seconds

A compact map of the exam before the critique begins.

What is NEET? India’s national entrance exam for undergraduate medicine.

Your NEET score is the only measure of your individual academic merit used in admission. No interview. No essay. No recommendations. No extracurricular review. Official NTA data

When do students take it? After Class XII, though many return for additional attempts.

In India, medical school starts right after high school—not after a separate four-year college degree, as in the United States.

What is the exam? 180 multiple-choice questions in 180 minutes.
What subjects? 90 Biology · 45 Chemistry · 45 Physics.
How is it scored? Correct +4 · Wrong −1 · Blank 0.
How often and where? Normally once a year, in person, on paper, at assigned exam centres.

If illness or one bad day derails the attempt, the next standard opportunity is normally a year later. One date, thousands of examination centres, and millions of candidates moving through the same system—in 2024, 4,750 centres in 571 cities, including 14 outside India.

Offered in 13 languages. Reservations and domicile rules determine which seats you can compete for. Within each eligible pool, candidates are ordered by NEET rank. The seat pools differ. The measuring stick does not: one exam score. Official NTA data

Not every seat is equally usable. Government medical colleges are subsidized. A private-college offer can cost far more than a family can pay. The real outcomes are: an affordable government seat, an unaffordable private offer, or no seat.

02 · The promise

One paper. One rank. Everyone gets a fair shot.

That is the promise—and why the system looks fair: no interview, no family name on the form, the same questions for every candidate, and a single list that looks like pure measurement.

What everyone sees

Same paper · Same clock · Same scoring

What the rank does not show

School · Language · Coaching · Geography · Family money · Years available

03 · The prize

Medicine is worth wanting. That is why families keep paying to remain in the race.

For many families, medicine represents professional stability, social standing, and the possibility of upward mobility. Families return because the prize is real.

In a Rajasthan court case, private-medical-college tuition for the relevant seats ranged from about ₹18.9 lakh to ₹25 lakh per year. The student bringing the case qualified for India’s Economically Weaker Section category, which uses a family-income ceiling of ₹8 lakh per year.

Annual tuition alone was therefore about 2.4 to 3.1 times the EWS income ceiling—not including living costs or the remaining years of the degree.

Why guessing can pay

A blind four-option guess has an expected value of +0.25 marks: one-in-four chance of +4, three-in-four chance of −1. Leaving blank scores 0 for sure.

Correct answer: A. EcoRI recognizes GAATTC when read in the 5′→3′ direction. Option C presents a different 5′→3′ sequence; option D is the recognition site for another enzyme, BamHI.

07 · Pay to stand still

Everyone runs harder. The seats do not move.

Coaching can help one student. That is why families buy it. When everyone who can pay responds, preparation becomes the entry fee rather than a lasting advantage. Students may learn more and scores may rise. But when the number of places stays fixed, everyone cannot gain rank.

By 2019–20, coaching was no longer an unusual advantage among Tamil Nadu’s admitted students: the Rajan Committee reported that about 99% had received it. Among admits, not all candidates

Coaching does not need to be useless for the system to be wasteful. It only needs to become necessary for standing still.

What the treadmill looks like among winners

Seven students from government schools in Madurai

Tamil Nadu reserves 7.5% of state medical places for students educated in government schools. In one 2025 report, all seven Madurai students who obtained places through that route were repeat test-takers who had also used private coaching. Six of the seven had parents working as daily-wage earners.

08 · Years as currency

The system rewards those who can survive another year inside it.

National attempt histories are not publicly available. Tamil Nadu provides one unusually detailed window into who obtained medical seats. Among students who actually entered MBBS programs in Tamil Nadu, the repeater share—people returning after leaving school to try again—rose from about 12.5% in 2016–17 to 71.4% in 2020–21.

Only 28.6% of the 2020–21 admitted cohort were students taking NEET in the same year they completed Class XII. The rest had already paid in time.

09 · The laundering Sources

Unequal lives go in. Merit comes out.

The leak broke the rules. The ordinary system shows why clean rules are not enough.

Millions pay in money, years, and fear for a structurally scarce prize. Privilege buys a longer runway and more chances to remain in the race. The rank list hides the price of the ticket. Then inequality returns wearing the language of merit.

A fair paper cannot make an unfair race fair.

A clean examination is necessary. It is not sufficient. Fixing the leak does not fix the race.
