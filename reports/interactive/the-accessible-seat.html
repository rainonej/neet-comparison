NEET · Common paper

NEET-UG · India · amid the 2026 protests

A fair paper is not a fair race.

NEET is India’s once-a-year national entrance exam for undergraduate medicine. More than two million students sit a three-hour multiple-choice test for roughly 130,000 MBBS seats.

A paper leak triggered the current protests. But suppose the paper had never leaked. Would the ordinary system be fair?

No. Scroll to see why ↓

01 · Orientation

NEET in 20 seconds

A compact map of the exam before the critique begins.

What is it?India’s national entrance exam for undergraduate medicine.
Who takes it?Students finishing secondary school, plus people returning after one or more years spent preparing again.
What happens?180 multiple-choice questions in 180 minutes.
What subjects?90 Biology · 45 Chemistry · 45 Physics.
How is it marked?Correct +4 · Wrong −1 · Blank 0 · Maximum 720.
How often?Normally one national examination each year.
Where and how?Pen and paper, on an OMR answer sheet, at assigned testing centres.
What does the score do?It produces a rank used in counselling to allocate medical-college seats.

This essay focuses on MBBS, India’s primary medical degree. Unlike the United States, students enter this medical program directly after secondary school rather than after a separate four-year college degree.

Not every seat is equally accessible. Government medical colleges are subsidized; a private-college offer may be financially impossible for the same family. The central outcome is not simply seat versus no seat. It is an affordable government seat versus an unaffordable private offer versus no seat.

02 · The promise

One paper. One rank. Everyone gets a fair shot.

That is the seduction: no interview, no family name on the form, the same questions for every candidate, and a single list that looks like pure measurement.

What everyone sees

Same paper · Same clock · Same scoring

What the rank does not show

School · Language · Coaching · Geography · Family money · Years available

03 · The prize

Medicine is worth wanting. That is why families keep paying to remain in the race.

An MBBS seat can mean a structured profession, social respect, financial security, and upward mobility for an entire family. People return because the prize is real—not because they are foolish.

The promise to the studentA respected profession and a stable future.
The promise to the familyA generation-changing return on sacrifice.

04 · Guaranteed disappointment

The system requires most candidates to lose.

In 2024, about 2.33 million candidates appeared for roughly 129,602 MBBS seats—about 18 candidates for every seat. Of those seats, about 63,859 were in government colleges and 65,743 in private ones. The tighter bottleneck is often the affordable government place.

NTA calls candidates who clear a minimum score “qualified.” That does not mean a seat exists for them.

40

Your guess: 40 out of 100.

The data: roughly 10 out of 100.

“Qualified” is not access. The word promises far more than the seats can deliver.

18 candidates. One chair.

The exam does not discover that seventeen students are unworthy. It begins with only one place for them.

A blind four-option guess has an expected value of +0.25 marks: one-in-four chance of +4, three-in-four chance of −1. Leaving blank scores 0 for sure.

06 · Unequal tickets

Before the exam measures the student, the system has already measured the family.

Two candidates can receive the same paper. Their runways into that paper are not the same.

Shorter runway

  • Studied in a language with fewer high-quality mock tests and coaching materials
  • Cannot relocate to Kota or another coaching hub
  • Cannot pay for full-time preparation
  • Needs to start earning rather than spend another drop year retaking
  • Cannot afford private-college tuition

Longer runway

  • Extensive exam-specific material in the student’s school language
  • Can relocate for residential coaching
  • Can study full-time
  • Family can finance another attempt
  • Private medical college remains a usable backup

Same paper on exam day · Different ticket to reach it

What specialized preparation can change

Jeevith Kumar

Jeevith, the son of a shepherd and a tailor, scored 548/600 in Class XII (Grade 12) but 193 on his first NEET attempt without private tuition. After outside help financed a year-long residential coaching program, he scored 664 on his second attempt.

07 · Pay to stand still

Everyone runs harder. The seats do not move.

Coaching can help one student. That is why families buy it. When everyone who can pay responds, preparation becomes the entry fee rather than a lasting advantage. Learning can rise in absolute terms while the admissions contest stays zero-sum in relative terms.

One candidate rises after buying preparation
1 seat
Others who can afford it respond
1 seat
Scores may rise across the group. The seat stays fixed.
1 seat
Some disappear when money or time runs out
1 seat

Coaching does not need to be useless for the system to be wasteful. It only needs to become necessary for standing still.

08 · Years as currency

The system rewards those who can survive another year inside it.

National attempt histories are not publicly available. Tamil Nadu provides one unusually detailed window into who obtained medical seats. Among its admitted MBBS cohorts, the repeater share—people returning after leaving school to try again—rose from about 12.5% in 2016–17 to 71.4% in 2020–21.

Only 28.6% of the 2020–21 admitted cohort were current-year students. The rest had already paid in time.

Close

The leak broke the rules. The ordinary system shows why clean rules are not enough.

Millions pay in money, years, and fear for a structurally scarce prize. Privilege buys a longer runway and more chances to remain in the race. The rank list hides the price of the ticket. Then inequality returns wearing the language of merit.

A fair paper cannot make an unfair race fair.

A clean examination is necessary. It is not sufficient. Fixing the leak does not fix the race.
